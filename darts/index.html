Finding π with darts

Where the darts come from

Every dart lands where this lab's quantum random number generator: a Crypta Labs QCicada, a small USB stick on a Raspberry Pi in Mark's house that measures tiny, unpredictable particles of light says. When the dot under the chart is green, that's what you're getting. If the stick is ever offline, the page quietly switches to your browser's random number maker and the dot turns yellow.

The trick

The board is a square with a circle drawn inside it, touching all four edges. If darts land at truly random spots, the share that lands inside the circle works out to π ÷ 4. Count them, multiply by 4, and you have π. More darts, closer answer.

Why it's called Monte Carlo

Named after the casino, it's the idea of solving a problem with lots of random guesses. Scientists use it for weather, finance, nuclear physics, chip design and AI, usually on big computer clusters.

Why the randomness matters

The method only works if the darts are truly random. Ordinary computers fake randomness with a formula. This lab uses a real quantum random number generator, so each dart's landing spot comes from a quantum measurement instead of math.

Don't expect perfection

Even perfectly random darts wobble. With 10,000 darts you'll usually match π to about two decimal places. Getting each extra digit takes roughly 100 times more darts.
