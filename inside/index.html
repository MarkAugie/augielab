From a particle of light to your screen

Six steps, a fraction of a second, all at room temperature.

1
✨

Light

Inside the stick, a tiny light source shines on a sensor. Light arrives as individual particles, photons, and exactly how many land in any instant is a quantum event: not just unknown, but undecided until it's measured.

2
🔬

The QCicada

A Crypta Labs QCicada, a USB stick, turns that photon noise into bits. It keeps checking itself with NIST health tests and stops if anything looks wrong.

3
⚗️

Refining

Raw sensor readings have small quirks. The stick runs them through SHA-256 (NIST SP 800-90B conditioning), so what comes out is evenly spread. See it in the Refinery.

4
🍓

Raspberry Pi 5

A small computer in Mark's house keeps 256 KB of quantum bytes ready, refills it constantly, and recovers by itself if the stick hiccups.

5
🔒

Secure tunnel

An encrypted Cloudflare Tunnel carries the bytes out. Nothing is opened to the internet at home, and a secret key means only this website can ask.

6
🎲

Your screen

The page you're on uses them to roll dice, shuffle cards, throw darts and split universes. If the generator is ever offline, pages quietly use your browser's generator and say so.

Prove it

Anyone can claim their numbers are quantum. This stick can back it up: it has its own secret signing key built in, and it signs numbers the moment it makes them. Your browser checks the signature right here, with no help from this website. Change even one byte and the check fails.

How it works: the stick signs with a private key that never leaves it. The public key above can only confirm signatures, never make them, so a matching signature means these exact bytes came out of this exact stick. Myth-busters

Questions people actually ask.

Doesn't this need to be cooled to near absolute zero?

No, and it's a great question, because some quantum machines do. Quantum computers keep their qubits a few thousandths of a degree above absolute zero, because any warmth would scramble the delicate states they calculate with.

A quantum random number generator does the opposite. It doesn't need to protect anything; it just measures something that quantum physics makes unpredictable. That works at room temperature, on a stick smaller than your thumb.

So is this a quantum computer?

No. A quantum computer calculates with qubits. This generator measures quantum events and turns the results into numbers. It's a quantum measuring device, which is why it fits in a USB port.

Isn't my computer's random number maker good enough?

For security, your browser's generator is excellent; banks rely on it. But it's a formula. Started from the same seed, it gives the same numbers every time, so in principle it's predictable. A quantum measurement isn't the result of any formula or seed. As far as physics knows, the outcome isn't decided until it happens.

For dice and card games you'd never notice the difference. That's what makes it fun: same odds, deeper story. Watch both kinds race in the Randomness Showdown.

How do you know it's really quantum?

Three ways. The physics: photon counts are a textbook quantum effect. The tests: the stick runs NIST health checks on itself constantly, and the numbers pass standard randomness tests. And the signature: the "Prove it" check above shows the bytes came from this device, unaltered.

How fast is it?

About 100,000 random bits a second, roughly 12,000 bytes. That's plenty for every page on this site at once. Big data-center quantum generators run far faster, billions of bits a second, but they cost a lot more than a USB stick.

What if it's unplugged, or the power goes out?

Every page notices within seconds and switches to your browser's generator, and the dot at the bottom of the page turns from green to yellow. When the generator comes back, pages switch back by themselves.

Could someone else use it or drain it?

Only this website holds the key that lets it ask the Pi for numbers, and the key never reaches your browser. Each page asks for small batches and waits politely if the generator is busy or offline.
