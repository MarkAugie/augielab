So… did you just split the universe?

The weird part of physics

At the tiniest scale, nature doesn't behave like everyday life. A single particle of light or energy doesn't "decide" what it will do until something measures it, and the result is truly random. Not hard to predict. Impossible to predict, even in principle.

The Many-Worlds idea

In 1957, physicist Hugh Everett proposed a wild answer to that randomness: nothing is ever chosen at all. Every possible outcome happens, and the universe branches so each one gets its own world. One you sees heads. Another you, just as real, sees tails.

What this site actually does

When you press the button, this site asks a real quantum random number generator for a number. No computer formula, no dice, no trick. Even number: Universe A. Odd number: Universe B.

The console is live

The instrument panel isn't decoration. The Photon rate needle shows how fast the quantum generator in Mark's lab is turning light into random bits right now (about 100,000 a second). Reserve shows how many fresh quantum numbers are waiting. The Generator and Self-test lamps are real checks: the stick tests itself constantly and stops if anything looks wrong. The Nixie tubes count every universe split on AugieLab, and yours (kept only on your own device).

The light show, explained

The animation is the most famous quantum experiment there is. Shine one single particle of light at a half-silvered mirror, and it has a 50/50 chance of going through or bouncing off. Physics says that until a detector measures it, it isn't on one path or the other. It's on both. Then a detector clicks, and only one answer is real. The generator in Mark's lab measures light in its own way, counting particles instead of using a mirror, but it's the same kind of unpredictable.

The picture of your universe

The glowing sphere is a map of everything we can see, with us in the middle. At the very center is the Sun with its planets, inside our home galaxy, the Milky Way. Around it are our neighbor galaxies, then the "cosmic web": billions of galaxies gathered into clusters and long glowing threads. The speckled, colorful ring at the edge is the oldest light there is, the afterglow of the Big Bang, which scientists have mapped from space with satellites like NASA's WMAP. Nothing can be seen beyond it.

Your picture is drawn fresh for every split, using your quantum reading to place every galaxy and speckle. No one has ever seen that exact universe before. When it splits, the two copies start out identical, as many-worlds says they would. Then one drifts away for good, because the two branches can never touch again.

Your universe ID

The code on your result (like #5C24-2EB9) is the actual quantum reading, written in hexadecimal. The first number decided your universe: even for A, odd for B. The same numbers also drew your universe's picture. Nobody else will ever get the same one. The "How do I know it's real?" link goes to a page where the generator signs its numbers so your browser can check they came from it.

A quantum lab at home

The number comes from a Crypta Labs QCicada, a small USB quantum random number generator that measures tiny, unpredictable particles of light. It's plugged into a Raspberry Pi in Mark's home lab near Portland, Oregon. If it's ever offline, the site asks a physics lab at the Australian National University instead, which measures the flickering energy of empty space itself. Same idea, same physics. The reading under your result says which one split your universe.

So is it real?

If Many-Worlds is right, yes. There's now another you out there living the other choice. If it's wrong, you still handed your decision to the laws of physics, which is pretty cool on its own. Scientists are still debating it, and no experiment can settle it yet.

Fun fact

By this theory, the universe already splits countless times every second on its own, from starlight, radioactive atoms, even the atoms in your body. You just picked one of those moments and attached your pizza to it.
